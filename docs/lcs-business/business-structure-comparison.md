## Why this document exists

Karen and Cece have talked about two ways to set up Lake Country Spanish: the
"founder + founding instructor" model that has been on paper since May, or a
50/50 co-ownership of the LLC. Both are valid. They lead to different rights,
different responsibilities, and different money.

This page is for **education only**. It does not recommend one. It lays out
both side by side so that the choice is made with the facts open.

## The two structures, in one sentence each

**A. Founder + founding instructor.** Karen owns the LCS LLC. Cece teaches
under the LCS brand as a 1099 independent contractor with the best ("founding")
revenue split. Karen makes the business decisions; Cece focuses on teaching.

**B. 50/50 co-ownership.** Karen and Cece are equal members of the LCS LLC.
Both own 50% of the company. Both share the profits 50/50. Both share the
business decisions 50/50. Both share the legal and financial responsibility
50/50.

## What jargon means

- **LLC member** — a legal owner of the LLC. Members share in the profit and
  the loss.
- **Distribution** — money the LLC pays to its members from its profit.
- **1099 contractor** — a worker who is not an owner. Gets paid for the work
  done, files their own taxes.
- **Operating agreement** — the written contract among the LLC members. Says
  who owns what, who decides what, what happens if a member leaves.
- **K-1** — a tax form a partnership sends to each owner once a year. Reports
  the owner's share of the partnership's profit.

## Side-by-side

| Topic | A. Founder + founding instructor | B. 50/50 co-ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the LLC | Karen (100%) | Karen 50%, Cece 50% |
| Who pays the LLC's startup costs | Karen alone | Both, equally |
| Who decides on prices, marketing, hiring, curriculum direction | Karen, after consulting founding instructors | Both, agreed together |
| What happens if Karen and Cece disagree | Karen's decision stands | The decision is stuck until they agree (this is called *deadlock*) |
| Money each gets from teaching | Each keeps the founding split: 80% of students they bring, 60% of students LCS brings | Same — the split for teaching work is separate from ownership |
| Money each gets from the business profit | Karen gets the LLC's profit (after costs) as the owner | Karen gets 50%, Cece gets 50% |
| Who is legally responsible if LCS gets sued | The LLC (Karen, as sole member, has the most exposure in practice) | The LLC (both members share exposure in practice) |
| What happens if one wants to leave | Karen stays as owner. The leaving instructor stops teaching. | The operating agreement must spell out how a member buys out the other, or how the LLC is wound down |
| Tax filing | Single-member LLC. Karen reports LCS profit on her personal taxes. Simpler. | Partnership tax return (Form 1065) every year. Each member gets a K-1. Cost: about $400–$800/year for a tax preparer. |
| Bookkeeping | One owner's draws | Tracked separately per member |
| Cost to set up | About $200 (WI LLC filing) + simple operating agreement | About $200 (WI LLC filing) + more careful operating agreement — typically $300–$800 with a lawyer if you want the deadlock and exit clauses done right |

## A money example

To make this concrete, here is what each person would earn in a starting-point
scenario. Both Karen and Cece teach 30 students each. Each student pays $200
per month. Every student in this example was brought by their own instructor
(so the 80/20 split applies).

| Where money comes from | Amount per month |
|---|---|
| Total student payments to LCS | $12,000 (60 students × $200) |
| Instructor share (80% of all student payments) | $9,600 — split: Karen $4,800 / Cece $4,800 |
| LCS share (20%) | $2,400 |
| LCS operating costs (Stripe fees, hosting, insurance, accounting, etc.) | about $1,400 |
| LCS profit | about $1,000 |

What each person takes home:

| | A. Founder + founding instructor | B. 50/50 co-ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Karen's teaching pay | $4,800 | $4,800 |
| Karen's owner distribution | $1,000 | $500 |
| **Karen total** | **$5,800/month** | **$5,300/month** |
| Cece's teaching pay | $4,800 | $4,800 |
| Cece's owner distribution | $0 (she is not an owner in model A) | $500 |
| **Cece total** | **$4,800/month** | **$5,300/month** |

**Difference per month: $500.** Over a year, that is $6,000.

In model A, Karen earns $1,000/month more than Cece because she owns the
business. In model B, the two earn the same. Cece earns $500/month more under
B than under A. Karen earns $500/month less under B than under A.

## What happens if LCS grows

The $500/month difference above is based on two instructors. If LCS grows to
six instructors with the same per-student math, LCS's monthly profit grows
toward $4,000/month.

- In model A, the entire $4,000 flows to Karen as the owner.
- In model B, each of the two co-owners gets $2,000.

The owner's share grows as LCS grows. This is the long-term value of being the
owner. It is also the reason owning takes more work and more risk.

## What each person is signing up for

**In model A**, Cece is teaching and growing her own student practice. She does
not pay startup costs. She does not handle taxes for the LLC. She is not on
the hook if LCS is sued. She can stop teaching at any time. Her income depends
on how many students she teaches and on Karen's pricing decisions.

**In model B**, both Karen and Cece are running a business together. Both
share the work that is not teaching: bookkeeping, taxes, insurance, vendor
contracts, hiring decisions, dispute resolution. Both fund startup costs. Both
have legal exposure as members. Decisions take longer because two people must
agree. The upside is that growth is shared 50/50.

## The risks each model carries

**Risks of model A:**

- Cece may feel she is contributing equally without owning equally. If she
  brings as many students and writes as much curriculum as Karen, the optics
  of "Karen owns it, Cece is a contractor" can strain the friendship later.
- The model depends on Karen continuing to do all the non-teaching work. If
  she steps back, the business has no second decider.

**Risks of model B:**

- **Deadlock.** When two people own a company 50/50 and disagree, nothing
  moves. This is the most common reason 50/50 partnerships fail. The fix is a
  well-written operating agreement with a tie-breaker (a neutral advisor, a
  buy-sell clause, a mediated process). It must be written *before* the
  disagreement happens.
- **Unequal contribution over time.** One member may end up doing more work
  than the other while both get the same money. The fix is to write into the
  operating agreement how unequal contributions are handled — for example, an
  hourly rate for non-teaching work that gets paid before profit is split.
- **Shared liability.** If one member makes a mistake (a contract violation, a
  student injury, a tax misstep), both members can be exposed. Good insurance
  helps. Clear roles help more.

## Hybrid options to know about

These do not have to be either/or. A few middle paths exist:

- **51/49 ownership instead of 50/50.** One member has the tie-breaking vote.
  Most of the upside of equal partnership; deadlock risk is eliminated.
- **Vesting.** A 50/50 split that is earned over time. If a co-owner leaves in
  the first two years, their ownership reverts to the other member.
- **Sweat-equity catch-up.** Whoever does more of the non-teaching work gets
  paid first from the LLC's share before profit is split.
- **Phantom equity or profits interest.** Cece gets a guaranteed share of
  future profits without being a legal member. Less tax complexity, less
  liability, but also less control.

Any of these is a valid conversation. Pick what matches how the two of you
actually want to work together day-to-day.

## Questions to talk about before deciding

1. **Who wants to do which work?** Teaching is one job. Bookkeeping, taxes,
   marketing, platform management, school outreach, vendor contracts — these
   are another job. Who wants which?

2. **How will we make a decision when we disagree?** Whichever model is
   chosen, this must be answered now. Not "we'll figure it out" — actually
   written down.

3. **What happens if one of us wants out in year 2?** Both models can handle
   this, but the rules must be in the operating agreement before it happens.

4. **How much money does each of us need to start?** Model A puts the LLC's
   startup costs on Karen. Model B splits them. The Wisconsin LLC fee, the
   operating agreement, the initial insurance — all of those need to be paid
   before the first student.

5. **What does "fair" look like to each of us?** If Cece is bringing as many
   students and writing as much curriculum as Karen, equal ownership may feel
   right. If Karen is fronting the capital, the legal risk, and the
   non-teaching work, the founder model may feel right. Talk about it before
   the lawyer drafts anything.

## What this document does not decide

Which structure is "better" depends on what each of you actually wants from
LCS. The math above shows the dollars. The friendship, the work styles, the
appetite for risk — those decide which structure fits.

Whatever you decide, the operating agreement must reflect it clearly. Reach
the decision together, then have a lawyer write it down before the first
student pays.
